## Market Definition

- **Target market**: inland intermodal logistics services in the Omaha / Council Bluffs MSA, focused on (1) marine container storage and gate operations, (2) transloading between marine container, rail car, bulk truck, and dry van, and (3) general and US Customs bonded warehousing.
- **Customer segments**:
  1. **Steamship lines and their inland equipment teams** (e.g., Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, ONE, Evergreen, Cosco) — they need approved inland depots for empties and laden boxes.
  2. **Ocean NVOCCs and freight forwarders** — they need flexible CY space and transload labor.
  3. **Ag exporters and originators** within ~150 miles — soybeans, DDGS, corn, pork, beef, hides, ethanol byproducts (Bunge, ADM, Cargill, CHS, Tyson, Smithfield, JBS, and the local elevator and crush network).
  4. **Import distributors** of consumer durables, appliances, e-bikes / e-scooters / EV components, furniture, machinery, and automotive parts moving from West Coast (LA/LB, Oakland, Seattle/Tacoma, Prince Rupert) and Gulf (Houston, Mobile, New Orleans) ports into the Midwest.
  5. **Regional 3PLs and brokers** needing overflow CY, transload, or bonded space.
- **Geography**: catchment radius of roughly 75 miles for drayage-anchored services and up to 250–350 miles for transload origination and bonded distribution (Iowa, Nebraska, eastern South Dakota, western Missouri, northern Kansas).
- **Time horizon**: 7-year operating plan (Year 0 site work, Year 1 CY/transload open, Year 2 standard warehouse, Year 3 bonded warehouse, Years 4–7 stabilized operations).
- **Included categories**: container yard (loaded and empty), gate operations, chassis pool services, container M&R light repair (heavy M&R to be evaluated as Phase 4), transloading (container ↔ bulk truck, container ↔ rail, bulk ↔ container), dry warehouse storage, bonded warehouse (CBP 19 CFR Part 19, likely **Class 3 — public bonded warehouse for storage of imported merchandise**, possibly Class 8 for cleaning / repacking; FTZ #64 subzone status to evaluate as an alternative), captive drayage via Mr. Xu's trucking company.
- **Excluded categories** (for now): refrigerated container storage requiring genset farms, hazmat / IMDG Class 1 storage, heavy chassis M&R, ocean / rail line-haul, customs brokerage (will partner rather than become a licensed broker).

## Why Omaha / Council Bluffs

- **Rail**: Union Pacific is headquartered in Omaha and operates the Council Bluffs Yard, one of the largest classification yards in the system. BNSF operates Gibson Yard and serves the corridor from Lincoln and Kansas City. Both serve the Midwest grain belt and West Coast / Gulf intermodal corridors.
- **Highway**: I-29 (north–south, Kansas City ↔ Sioux Falls ↔ Fargo), I-80 (east–west, transcontinental), and I-680 / I-480 form a dense highway node. The site fronts I-29, which is the spine for ag exports moving toward Gulf ports and for north-south domestic drayage.
- **Ag**: Iowa is the #1 US soybean producer (and Nebraska is in the top 5). The Council Bluffs Bunge complex is a major crush and export feeder. Soybean and DDGS exports increasingly move in containers when bulk vessel space and rail car availability are tight, especially for specialty (non-GMO, organic, identity-preserved) shipments.
- **Imports**: The Omaha MSA is a regional consumption and distribution node for the upper Midwest; appliances, furniture, e-mobility, and automotive parts arrive predominantly from West Coast ports and ship to dealers, big-box retailers, and regional DCs.
- **Empty-box imbalance**: Inland Midwest origins are chronically short of empties available for export loading. An independent CY with carrier relationships solves an availability problem for ag exporters and an asset-utilization problem for carriers simultaneously.

### Global Container Transportation Map

The practical container loop to test is:

1. China / Northeast Asia origin ports load consumer goods, machinery, components, furniture, e-mobility goods, and other containerized imports.
2. Containers land at West Coast gateways, especially Los Angeles / Long Beach, then move inland by rail/intermodal or trucked regional linehaul.
3. Containers reach Omaha / Council Bluffs and the upper Midwest, where import cargo is unloaded for regional distribution.
4. Empty boxes can either return empty to coastal ports or be repositioned into Midwest export programs.
5. Iowa / Nebraska ag and feed exporters can use those boxes for containerized soybeans, DDGS, soybean meal, specialty grains, and related products.

This loop is the core map logic: **the site is most useful where import box disposition and export empty-box demand meet**.

### Data-Backed Niche Finding

The niche is narrower than "general warehouse near Omaha." Based on the second-look evidence, the strongest internal hypothesis is:

> A carrier-named, off-dock container yard and food/feed-grade transload node near Bunge Ave / I-29, with optional bonded storage for China/Asia-origin importers.

Evidence signals:

- **China remains the largest U.S. containerized oceanborne trading partner**. The FMC FY2025 annual report lists China (PRC) at **11,749,926 TEUs**, ahead of Vietnam, India, South Korea, and Thailand.
- **Los Angeles remains a major Northeast Asia gateway**. Port of Los Angeles 2025 Facts and Figures lists China/Hong Kong as its top trading partner and Northeast Asia as the largest trade-route region by share.
- **Containerized ag export demand is real but commodity-specific**. USDA AMS reported 2024 U.S. containerized grain shipments of **15.2 mmt**, up **13%** from 2023. DDGS and soybeans were the largest containerized grain commodities, at **5.1 mmt each**, followed by soybean meal and corn.
- **Iowa and Nebraska form a large nearby export base**. USTR state profiles list Iowa as the #2 U.S. agricultural exporting state in 2024 at **$13.7B**, including soybeans, pork, corn, feeds/feed grains, and soybean meal. Nebraska was #5 at **$8.2B**, including beef/veal, corn, soybeans, feeds/feed grains, and processed grain products.
- **Bonded storage is a plausible import-side add-on, not the primary proof point yet**. 19 CFR 19.1 supports Class 3 public bonded warehouses for imported merchandise and Class 8 manipulation for cleaning, sorting, repacking, or condition changes under Customs supervision. USTR's 2024 Section 301 action confirms continued tariff-policy complexity around China-origin goods. However, bonded demand still needs importer and customs-broker interviews.

## Sizing Logic (Draft — show your math)

**Step 1 — Container yard slot capacity.**

- Usable CY area after gates, drives, M&R, and stacking aisles: ~13–16 acres of the 21-acre site.
- At a conservative 80 TEU per usable acre (stacked 3-high empties; lower for loaded) the static slot inventory is **~1,000–1,300 TEU**.
- At a 5-day average dwell with 80% utilization, annual throughput is in the range of **~58,000–76,000 TEU per year**. Mix of empty and loaded changes the dwell and price realization.

**Step 2 — Transload throughput.**

- Two transload doors (one bulk-to-container for ag, one container-to-truck for imports) with 8 container-equivalents per shift and one shift per day equals **~16 containers per day × ~250 working days = ~4,000 containers per year** at maturity.
- With seasonal ag peaks (Sep–Feb harvest export window) actual throughput can run 25–40 boxes per day during peak with overtime.

**Step 3 — Warehouse demand.**

- Phase 2 standard warehouse target: ~80,000–120,000 sq ft. At $0.65 / sq ft / month blended (rack + floor) revenue, that is **$0.6M–$0.94M per month at full occupancy**, before transactional fees (in/out, kitting, labeling).
- Phase 3 bonded warehouse target: ~30,000–50,000 sq ft at a +25–40% premium over standard rates.

**Step 4 — Revenue envelope.**

- These three layers, combined with captive drayage, frame a stabilized Year 4 revenue range of **$18M–$28M**. Concentrate this in `doc/FINANCIAL_MODEL_NOTES.md` and confirm with Mr. Xu before publishing externally.

## Demand Drivers And Headwinds

**Drivers**:

- Continued growth in containerized ag exports, especially specialty soy and DDGS to Asia and Mexico.
- E-commerce inventory regionalization driving demand for inland 3PL and bonded storage capacity outside the legacy LA / IE / Chicago / Dallas megaclusters.
- Carrier strategies favoring inland depot networks to reduce empty-equipment imbalance penalties.
- Tariff volatility (Section 232, 301, IEEPA-based, AD/CVD) makes deferred-duty / bonded storage and FTZ status more economically valuable than in low-tariff periods.
- USMCA-driven nearshoring is increasing cross-border truck volume; bonded operations can support in-bond moves (T&E, IE).

**Headwinds**:

- Class I railroads compete directly through their own intermodal terminals and ramp services; partnership terms must be negotiated.
- Soybean export volume is tied to US-China trade policy and Brazilian harvest cycles.
- Truck driver availability and HOS rules constrain regional drayage capacity.
- CBP bonded warehouse approval and FTZ subzone activation have long lead times (6–12+ months) and require strong internal controls.
- Capital cost of paving and equipment is high and sensitive to interest rates.
